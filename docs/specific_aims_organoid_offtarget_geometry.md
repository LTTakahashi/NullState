# Convergent or Lineage-Specific? The Geometry of Off-Target Cell States Across Human Organoid Systems

## SPECIFIC AIMS

Human organoids have become central tools for disease modeling, drug screening, and developmental biology, yet they reproducibly generate off-target cell populations — mesenchymal cells in neural organoids, cartilage and skeletal muscle in kidney organoids, and analogous contaminants across endodermal systems — that do not correspond to the intended tissue. These populations compromise model fidelity and are routinely treated as system-specific artifacts to be minimized protocol by protocol. Whether they share a deeper structure across organ systems is unknown. When a differentiating cell fails to reach or hold its intended lineage, does it converge toward a small set of default states regardless of developmental origin, or does each lineage fail in a characteristic, lineage-bound way? The answer bears directly on the nature of cellular identity and on whether organoid fidelity can be improved by general versus system-specific interventions.

This question has been inaccessible because off-target populations were characterized within individual organoid systems by separate research communities. The recent germ-layer organoid atlases — the Human Endoderm Organoid Cell Atlas (HEOCA), the Human Neural Organoid Cell Atlas (HNOCA), and large-scale kidney organoid benchmarks — now provide integrated, primary-tissue-anchored references within each germ layer, accessible through CELLxGENE. The cross-germ-layer comparison required to test convergence has not been performed, in part because no single group spans all three germ layers, and in part because the atlases were integrated under incompatible assumptions: differing highly variable gene (HVG) selections, batch-correction algorithms (scVI vs. scPoli), and coordinate systems. The central analytical obstacle, documented within HNOCA, is a universal *in-vitro* signature — driven by culture media, growth factors, and glycolytic metabolism — that dominates the latent space and would make all off-target populations appear convergent for a trivial, non-biological reason. **Our central hypothesis is that off-target states form a structured mixture: a subset converging across germ layers toward shared default states, and a subset that is lineage-bound.** We will test this through three aims.

**Aim 1. Construct a harmonized cross-germ-layer reference space and off-target ontology.** We will re-integrate from raw count matrices rather than rely on the incompatible published latent spaces, drawing off-target clusters and their nearest on-target neighbors from HEOCA, HNOCA, and kidney organoid datasets. All cells will be mapped to fetal and adult primary references in CELLxGENE so that off-target states can be defined operationally, by their deviation from primary counterparts. A unified HVG selection spanning ectoderm, endoderm, and mesoderm will be built to avoid biasing the manifold toward any single lineage. *Outcome:* a single coordinate system and a consistent, cross-system definition of what constitutes an off-target population.

**Aim 2. Disentangle the in-vitro stress signature from lineage biology by contrastive subspace separation.** We will learn a representation that partitions variation into a shared *in-vitro* subspace (z_iv), common to all organoid cells regardless of lineage, and a *lineage* subspace (z_lin). The primary method is a contrastive latent-variable model (contrastiveVI) trained with primary on-target cells as background and organoid on-target cells as target, so that the salient latent absorbs the organoid-specific in-vitro signature while the shared latent retains lineage identity; the in-vitro axis is thus learned from identity-matched on-target cells and applied to off-target cells without anchoring them to a primary identity (the calibration-without-anchoring strategy of the Innovation section). We adopt the contrastive formulation rather than adversarial subspace separation because its objective is stable and its shared/salient partition is identifiable, whereas adversarial minimax training is unstable and its solution is not; an adversary that attempts to predict organoid-versus-primary origin from z_lin is retained only as a post-hoc verification check (disentanglement is confirmed when it cannot), and conditional flow-matching is retained as a benchmarked alternative. Disentanglement will be verified by confirming that z_iv predicts culture conditions while z_lin predicts cell identity but not organoid origin. *Outcome:* a representation in which off-target states can be compared on lineage signal alone, with the trivial "grown-in-a-dish" confound removed with mathematical equity across all three germ layers.

**Aim 3. Characterize and validate the geometry of off-target convergence.** On the disentangled manifold, we will quantify the distance between residual off-target state distributions across germ layers using complementary measures — Earth Mover's Distance, cosine similarity of state-shift vectors, and persistent homology to capture topological structure robust to batch noise. Each off-target state will be classified as universal (convergent across germ layers) or lineage-bound. Findings will be validated on independent organoid datasets held out from the integration, testing whether the convergence pattern replicates rather than reflecting the specific datasets used to build the reference. *Outcome:* a quantitative map of which off-target states are shared across the body plan and which are lineage-specific.

**Impact.** This work delivers the first cross-system framework for diagnosing failed differentiation in organoids. If off-target states converge, we identify a default state that cells adopt when lineage commitment fails — a finding about the architecture of cellular identity, and a single target for correcting contaminating populations across many organoid systems at once. If they diverge, we map the characteristic failure boundary of each germ layer, enabling lineage-specific protocol correction; this null is not a failure but a landmark descriptive resource. Either way, the disentanglement method developed in Aim 2 addresses a confound that currently limits single-cell analysis across the entire organoid field.
