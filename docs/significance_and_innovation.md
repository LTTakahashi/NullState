# Significance and Innovation

## Significance

Human organoids have become a primary substrate for disease modeling, drug screening, developmental biology, and the earliest stages of regenerative medicine. Their entire value rests on a single premise: that an organoid recapitulates the biology of the tissue it is built to model. That premise is systematically undermined by a failure every organoid protocol shares — the reproducible generation of off-target cells that do not belong to the intended lineage. Across neural, kidney, and endodermal systems alike, mesenchymal and stromal populations arise unbidden, routinely comprising 20–30% of an organoid's mass. This "mesenchyme tax" contaminates every downstream use: it dilutes drug-screening signal, confounds disease phenotypes, and is a safety liability for any cell product intended for transplant. It is, in practical terms, the universal tax on organoid engineering.

The field currently treats this failure as a collection of local engineering problems. Each organ-specific community tunes its own differentiation protocol — its own media, factors, and timing — to suppress its own contamination, in isolation from the others. The result is enormous duplicated effort against what may be a single underlying phenomenon, and a fidelity ceiling that no individual protocol adjustment has lifted. The structure of the field enforces this blindness: organ-specific laboratories and germ-layer-specific reference atlases mean no one is positioned to ask whether brain-organoid mesenchyme and kidney-organoid mesenchyme are, in fact, the same failure. The question that matters most has gone unasked because no single group can see across the silos.

This proposal asks it directly: when differentiation fails, do cells across all three germ layers collapse toward a shared default state, or does each lineage fail in its own way? The answer carries two distinct kinds of weight. **Practically**, if the pan-organoid failure has a single transcriptomic attractor with definable coordinates, the field's problem transforms from fifty separate optimization efforts into one shared target — a defined state against which a single corrective intervention, a drug or ligand that represses or selectively eliminates it, could be screened once and deployed across every organoid system. **Fundamentally**, the existence of such an attractor would be evidence that cellular identity, when it fails, does not dissolve at random but converges toward a small set of default configurations — a claim about the architecture of cellular identity that reaches beyond organoids into development, dedifferentiation, and disease.

Critically, the project is built so that both answers advance the field. A shared attractor yields a unified engineering target and a principle of identity collapse. A divergent result yields the first systematic map of how each developmental program fails, equally actionable for lineage-specific correction. And because the central question is resolved definitively for the most universal and consequential off-target population — off-target mesenchyme, which is abundant in every system — the proposal delivers a definitive resource regardless of which way the biology falls. This is not a study that succeeds only if a hoped-for result appears; it is a study that produces a usable answer about the universal tax on organoid biology either way.

## Innovation

**Conceptual.** This is the first treatment of organoid off-target failure as a potentially shared, cross-germ-layer phenomenon rather than a set of system-specific artifacts. It performs the horizontal analysis the field's siloed structure has prevented, repurposing the recently completed germ-layer organoid atlases as inputs to a question none of them was constructed to answer.

**Methodological — calibration without anchoring.** The enabling innovation resolves a trap that would defeat any naive application of domain-adaptation methods here. Aligning organoid cells to primary references with a standard adversarial approach forces off-target populations — which have no primary counterpart — to collapse onto the nearest reference identity, destroying the aberrant geometry the study exists to measure (negative transfer under label shift). Our solution learns the in-vitro culture signature exclusively from identity-matched on-target cells, then freezes and applies it to off-target cells, removing the culture artifact without imposing a primary anchor. This converts the on-target cells from a passive control into the calibration instrument for the entire assay, and the strategy generalizes to any setting requiring removal of a technical confound from a population that has no clean reference.

**Statistical — banning the artifact.** The geometric comparison rests on a standard of statistical hygiene rare in single-cell work: dimension-robust optimal transport (sliced-Wasserstein and debiased Sinkhorn divergence) that escapes the curse of dimensionality fatal to exact transport in latent space; an empirically determined minimum sample size fixed by rarefaction; and a matched-N null floor that prevents sample-size inflation from being read as biological convergence. A maturation-matched control further separates a genuine failure-default attractor from the trivial similarity of immature cells — neutralizing the single most likely false-positive interpretation. Together these form a reusable framework for rigorous geometric comparison of single-cell populations, applicable well beyond this study.

In sum, the proposal reframes a universal and costly failure of organoid biology as a single answerable question, introduces a calibration method that makes the question tractable without corrupting its object, and answers it with a degree of statistical rigor that the conclusion — convergent or divergent — can be trusted and built upon.
